When I think about embedded systems in my home, my first thought is my thermostat, specifically one made by Resideo, a company that was spun off from Honeywell. I would consider the thermostat a small standalone embedded system because its hardware and software are built primarily for one specific purpose: monitoring and controlling the temperature of a home. Even though a modern thermostat can connect to Wi-Fi and communicate with other devices, its core HVAC control functions can still operate locally without constantly relying on another computer.

What makes the Resideo thermostat interesting to me is how much information a relatively small embedded system can process. Modern thermostats can use temperature, motion, occupancy, and proximity sensors to make decisions about heating and cooling. Different areas of a home also heat and cool differently. A smaller room might reach its target temperature much faster than a large open room. By observing temperature changes and occupancy patterns over time, a smart system can adjust when and how the HVAC system operates rather than simply turning the heat on and off at a fixed temperature.

I think this is a good example of how embedded systems impact society. We are going to see more devices combine sensors, software, and data to maximize efficiency without requiring people to constantly control them. A thermostat may seem like a small example, but if millions of homes can automatically reduce unnecessary heating and cooling, those small improvements can add up to significant reductions in energy consumption and costs. To me, that is what makes emerging embedded systems interesting: even a small computer designed for one purpose can have a much larger impact when it is used everywhere.
